# English guide

Use these patterns as evidence, not automatic replacement rules. A word or construction may be correct in context.

## Content patterns

- **Puffery:** Replace claims such as "pivotal moment," "testament to," or "indelible mark" with the event, mechanism, or consequence.
- **Promotional language:** Replace unsupported praise such as "groundbreaking," "renowned," "breathtaking," or "must-visit" with a neutral description or a supported fact.
- **Vague attribution:** Name the source behind "experts believe" or "reports suggest." If the source text does not identify one, preserve the uncertainty without fabricating attribution.
- **Superficial participles:** Check trailing phrases such as "highlighting," "showcasing," and "ensuring." Remove them when they merely restate the sentence; expand them only with information already present.
- **Formulaic tension:** Replace "despite challenges, it continues to thrive" and similar templates with the specific obstacle and outcome.
- **Generic significance:** Replace claims that something "matters," "changes everything," or "sets the stage" with the affected person, decision, or measurable result.

## Language patterns

- Prefer plain verbs to inflated constructions: "is" or "has" often beats "serves as," "stands as," "boasts," or "features."
- Prefer plain words when precision is unchanged: "use" for "utilize," "help" for "facilitate," and "if" for "in the event that."
- Cut filler such as "it is important to note that," "in order to," and "due to the fact that."
- Reduce stacked hedges. Preserve the source's uncertainty, but express it once.
- Avoid synonym cycling. Repeating the accurate term is clearer than rotating through near-synonyms.
- Check abstract metaphors such as "substrate," "wedge," "vector," "nexus," "bedrock," "scaffolding," "north star," and "flywheel." Keep genuine domain terms; replace decorative uses with the actual mechanism.
- Replace unsupported adverbs with a stronger verb, a number, or nothing.
- Prefer active voice when the actor matters. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or intentionally backgrounded.

## Structure and rhythm

- Break dense sentences when a reader must backtrack. Keep related clauses together when splitting would make the prose choppy.
- Vary sentence length naturally. Do not manufacture fragments merely to appear human.
- Remove forced groups of three, false "from X to Y" ranges, and "not just X, but Y" framing when they add no meaning.
- Use headings, lists, bold text, colons, parentheses, dashes, and quotation marks according to the document's style. Fix overuse rather than banning the punctuation or structure.
- Convert bold-label lists into prose when every label repeats the sentence that follows. Keep lists when the items are genuinely parallel or easier to scan.
- Prefer sentence-case headings unless the publication's style requires title case.

## Communication artifacts

- Remove chatbot preambles and closers such as "Of course," "I hope this helps," and "Let me know if you have questions" when they are not part of the intended voice.
- Replace reflexive agreement such as "Great question" or "You're absolutely right" with the answer or evidence.
- Remove cutoff disclaimers that merely announce missing detail. State the known limit directly.
- Avoid generic conclusions such as "the future looks bright." End on the last concrete implication or action.

## Final check

- Does every sentence provide a fact, instruction, reason, example, or supported reaction?
- Could a sentence appear unchanged in an unrelated project's copy? If so, make it specific or cut it.
- Did the edit preserve technical terms, exact claims, uncertainty, and the author's actual position?
- Does the passage sound like one person with a consistent vocabulary rather than a thesaurus-driven composite?
